## Chapter 41: Irregular Verb Conjugation

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Apply the six major stem-change rules automatically so conjugated forms are always correct.
**Scenario**: A learner tries to say 'the weather is hot / I know / please help me' and produces wrong forms; this chapter fixes the root cause by providing explicit rules and high-frequency drills.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-120 (ㅂ 불규칙: ㅂ → 워, e.g. 춥다 → 추워요) — exceptions: 잡다, 입다
- pattern-121 (ㄷ 불규칙: ㄷ → ㄹ, e.g. 듣다 → 들어요) — exceptions: 받다, 믿다
- pattern-122 (ㅅ 불규칙: ㅅ drops, e.g. 짓다 → 지어요) — exceptions: 웃다, 씻다
- pattern-136 (ㄹ-탈락: ㄹ drops before ㄴ/ㅂ/ㅅ, e.g. 알다 → 아는/압니다)
- pattern-124 (ㅎ 불규칙: ㅎ + vowel → 애요, e.g. 빨갛다 → 빨개요)
- pattern-123 (르 불규칙: 르 → ㄹㄹ, e.g. 모르다 → 몰라요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: High-frequency irregular verbs: weather (덥다/춥다), emotions (슬프다/기쁘다), senses (아프다), knowledge (알다/모르다), appearance (빨갛다/파랗다/노랗다), daily actions (걷다/듣다/짓다)
**Cultural Note**: Korean dictionaries list verb stems in their dictionary (plain) form, not the conjugated form. When you see 어렵다 in a dictionary, you will never encounter 어렵어요 in the real world — only 어려워요. Knowing the six rules means you can predict spoken Korean from written Korean.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 5, 11, 17
**Rationale**: Irregular conjugation is the single biggest source of A2 fossilization errors. Learners produce 덥어요 / 듣아요 / 모르아요 constantly. Without a dedicated chapter the errors persist indefinitely because the patterns are not predictable from the dictionary form.

## Chapter 42: Particle Expansion

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Use the full set of A2 particles to express 'also', 'only', possession, recipient, togetherness, 'nothing but', and 'at least'.
**Scenario**: Talking about weekend plans: who went with whom, whose bag it was, giving something to someone, having no choice but to study.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-139 (N도: also/too/even)
- pattern-140 (N만: only/just)
- pattern-118 (N의: possession/genitive)
- pattern-108 (N에게/한테/께: dative recipient particles)
- pattern-109 (N와/과/하고/(이)랑: comitative particles)
- pattern-110 (N밖에 + negation: nothing but)
- pattern-111 (N이나: as many as / surprising quantity)
- pattern-112 (N이라도: at least / concessive minimum)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Relationships and everyday objects: friends/family/teachers as recipients, possessions, quantities, daily items
**Cultural Note**: Korean particles are post-positional — they always follow the noun they modify. In rapid casual speech, particles are frequently dropped entirely (저 갔어 for 저는/제가 갔어요). Understanding when a particle is absent but implied is a key listening skill.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 3, 4, 5, 6
**Rationale**: Korean's particle system is its core grammar mechanism. Learners who know only 은/는/이/가/을/를/에/에서 cannot express 'me too', 'only this', 'to someone', or 'nothing but', forcing them into awkward circumlocutions. These particles are all encountered in everyday conversation at A2.

## Chapter 43: Counters and Classifiers

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Count people, objects, animals, sheets, cups, and occurrences using the correct classifier and number system.
**Scenario**: Ordering at a café, shopping for clothes, describing a family, and counting how many times something happened.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-119 (counter words: 개/명/분/권/잔/마리/장/벌/켤레/대/송이/채/번)
- native number contractions before counters: 하나→한, 둘→두, 셋→세, 넷→네, 스물→스무
- Two number systems: Sino-Korean for dates/money/minutes/floors; native Korean for most everyday counts
- Counter word order: Noun + Number + Classifier (사과 두 개 주세요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Shopping items, food and drink, animals, clothing, transport, buildings, and event frequency
**Cultural Note**: Koreans use the same counter 번 for both ordinal position (첫 번째 = first time) and frequency (한 번 = once, 두 번 = twice). When you say 몇 번이에요? to an airline attendant, they hear 'what seat number?'; the same phrase to a friend means 'how many times?'. Context and particle cue the listener.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 5, 7, 20
**Rationale**: Mismatched counters (saying 사람 한 개 instead of 한 명) mark a speaker as a raw beginner regardless of other competencies. Ch20 addresses numbers but not the classifier system — this chapter fills that gap.

## Chapter 44: Pronunciation Rules

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Predict and produce the spoken form of any written Korean word by applying the four core phonological rules.
**Scenario**: A learner reads 학교 as [hak-gyo] and 먹어요 as [meok-eo-yo]; native speakers cannot parse it; this chapter closes the gap by teaching the systematic rules.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-116 (연음 liaison: coda consonant moves to next vowel onset — 먹어요 → [머거요])
- pattern-117 (경음화 tensification: plain stops tense after blocked codas — 학교 → [학꾜])
- pattern-118 in phonology context (비음화 nasalization: stops → nasals before ㄴ/ㅁ — 국민 → [궁민])
- pattern-119 in phonology context (격음화 aspiration: stop + ㅎ → aspirate — 좋다 → [조타])
- new pattern needed: 연음 rule (받침 + 모음 → resyllabification)
- new pattern needed: 경음화 (ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ after blocked coda → tense)
- new pattern needed: 비음화 (coda ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ → ㅇ/ㄴ/ㅁ before nasal)
- new pattern needed: 격음화 (stop + ㅎ → aspirate)
**Vocabulary Domain**: High-frequency words that trigger these rules: common verb-ending combinations (먹고, 먹는, 먹어요), compound nouns (학교, 국민, 국밥), verbs with ㅎ (좋다, 않다, 넣다), and the formal ending 합니다
**Cultural Note**: Koreans learn romanization (로마자 표기법) in school, but they use it only for official signage and passports. In daily life, Koreans write in Hangul and their brains hear the phonological output, not the written input. When a Korean reads 먹어요, they automatically hear [머거요]; this chapter gives learners that same automatic map.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 1, 5, 17
**Rationale**: Ch01 covers Hangul writing but not phonology. The gap causes A2 learners to read written Korean with robotic letter-by-letter pronunciation. Liaison alone affects virtually every verb conjugation used in speech. These four rules together cover over 95% of pronunciation discrepancies in everyday speech.

## Chapter 45: Negation Deep Dive

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Distinguish all four negation strategies and choose the correct one based on ability, choice, and politeness register.
**Scenario**: Declining an invitation, explaining a dietary restriction, and saying something cannot be done versus will not be done.
**Key Grammar**:
- Short negation 안 V (volitional/habitual — 안 먹어요 = I don't eat by choice)
- Short negation 못 V (inability — 못 가요 = I can't go)
- pattern-120 in negation context (V-지 않다: long-form volitional negation — 먹지 않아요 = 안 먹어요)
- pattern-121 in negation context (V-지 못하다: long-form inability — 가지 못해요 = 못 가요)
- N하다 compound verb negation rule: 공부 안 해요 (NOT 안 공부해요)
- Adjective negation: 안 A / A-지 않다; 못 does not apply to pure adjectives
- 아니다 vs 없다 vs 안/못: copula negation vs existence negation vs action negation
- new pattern needed: V-지 않다 (long-form volitional)
- new pattern needed: V-지 못하다 (long-form inability)
- new pattern needed: N 안/못 하다 (N하다 compound split rule)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Daily refusal/inability verbs (먹다/가다/하다/알다/오다), dietary and allergy language, social declining phrases, compound N하다 verbs (공부/운동/요리/전화 하다)
**Cultural Note**: Koreans often decline invitations softly rather than with a direct 못 가요. The phrase 좀 어려울 것 같아요 ('it seems a bit difficult') is a common face-saving way to decline without specifying whether the reason is inability or unwillingness. Being able to hear and use this indirect negation is an important social skill.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 5, 8, 9
**Rationale**: Negation is among the first things learners need but the 안/못 distinction, the 지 않다 form, and the N하다 split rule are reliably mis-taught or under-taught. Learners plateau with one strategy (usually 안) and over-apply it, producing unnatural or wrong sentences.

## Chapter 46: 이다 Deep Dive

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Use 이다 in past tense, as a connective, as a reason clause, and as a concessive/minimal-option marker.
**Scenario**: Explaining a past identity, giving a reason based on what something is, connecting information about a noun, and expressing acceptance of a less-than-ideal option.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-141 (N이었어요/였어요: past tense of 이다)
- N이/가 아니었어요 (negation past of 이다)
- pattern-142 (N인데: noun background clause)
- pattern-143 (N이라서/라서: because it is N)
- pattern-144 (N이니까/니까: since it is N, emphatic reason)
- N이라도/라도: at least (concessive minimum, from pattern-112)
- Formal equivalents: N입니다 / N이었습니다
**Vocabulary Domain**: Identity and role nouns (학생/직원/외국인/초보자/선생님/의사), time nouns for reason clauses (주말/평일/휴가/아침), social discount/exception contexts
**Cultural Note**: In Korean social interaction, stating your identity/role often serves as a reason-giving shorthand: 학생이라서요 ('because I'm a student') fully justifies a discount request or explains limited knowledge. The copula 이다 is doing a lot of pragmatic work here — it is not just a linking verb but a social explanation tool.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 3, 11, 17, 30
**Rationale**: 이다 is the most frequent verb in Korean and its connective and reason forms (인데/이라서/이니까) appear in virtually every natural conversation. Learners need to understand that these are all morphological derivations of 이다, not separate patterns, to generalize them correctly. Past tense 이었어요 is also frequently absent from learner production.

## Chapter 47: Family and Honorific Kinship Terms

**Level**: A2
**Goal**: Name and address family members correctly in both plain and honorific registers, and talk about relationships.
**Scenario**: Introducing your family to a Korean colleague, asking about their parents, and discussing a friend's wedding.
**Key Grammar**:
- Plain (my family) vs. honorific (other's family) vocabulary: 아버지/아버님, 어머니/어머님, 할아버지/할아버님, 딸/따님, 아들/아드님
- In-group humbling: use plain forms for your own family when speaking to outsiders
- Address terms vs. reference terms: 오빠/언니 (women's older sibling terms), 형/누나 (men's older sibling terms), 남동생/여동생 (younger siblings)
- pattern-145 (저희 가족은 ~ 명이에요: describing family size)
- pattern-146 (N께서 V으시다: honorific subject particle with -(으)시-)
- 드리다 (humble 'give' to a senior, replacing 주다)
- Relationship vocabulary: 남자친구/여자친구, 약혼자, 결혼하다/이혼하다
**Vocabulary Domain**: Nuclear and extended family terms (both registers), address terms for siblings by speaker gender, relationship milestones, polite expressions used in family introductions
**Cultural Note**: Korean sibling address terms are gendered by the speaker, not the sibling — a woman calls her older brother 오빠, but a man calls his older brother 형. Getting this wrong is a socially notable error in Korea. The terms 형/오빠/언니/누나 are also widely used as friendly address terms for peers who are slightly older, not just biological siblings.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 3, 4, 20, 34
**Rationale**: Korean kinship vocabulary is uniquely complex: terms differ by the speaker's gender, by whether you're talking about your own family or someone else's, and by formality. Family introductions are among the most common conversation topics at A2, and misusing honorific vs. plain family terms causes genuine social awkwardness.

## Chapter 48: Health and Body

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Describe physical symptoms, navigate a clinic or pharmacy, and follow a doctor's basic instructions.
**Scenario**: A learner visits a neighborhood health clinic (동네 병원) with stomach pain, explains symptoms to the receptionist and doctor, and picks up medication at the attached pharmacy.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-011 (V아/어야 해요: you need to... — 약을 먹어야 해요)
- pattern-054 (V-는 바람에: unexpectedly because of... — for sudden illness onset)
- N 때문에 (because of — explaining cause of pain)
- V으니까 (since/because — giving reasons to doctor)
- V아서/어서 (sequential/cause: 많이 먹어서 배가 아파요)
- V-는 중이에요 (currently ongoing: 약을 먹는 중이에요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 머리/목/배/등/팔/다리/손/발, 열이 나다/기침이 나다/콧물이 나다/두통, 처방전/약사/의사, 하루 3번/식후 30분 (medication labels)
**Cultural Note**: Korean clinics are highly specialized and very fast: a typical 동네 내과 visit lasts under ten minutes. Patients state symptoms concisely to the receptionist before seeing the doctor. Pharmacies attached to clinics dispense pre-packed medication by dose, so learners must know how to read the label (하루 3번, 식후 30분).
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 17, 26, 30, 31
**Rationale**: Health emergencies are high-stakes and time-sensitive. B1 learners have the grammar infrastructure (causal connectors, obligation forms) to form medically coherent sentences but lack the domain vocabulary. Clinic and pharmacy visits are among the first real-world crises a newcomer faces.

## Chapter 49: Housing and Daily Living

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Describe a home's layout, discuss neighborhood amenities, and handle basic landlord/real-estate interactions.
**Scenario**: A newcomer views a 원룸 through a 부동산 (real-estate agency), asks about the deposit system and utilities, then describes the apartment to a friend over the phone.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-002 (N이/가 있어요: describing amenities present)
- pattern-003 (N이/가 없어요: describing what is missing)
- A-ㄴ/은 N modifier (넓은 방, 조용한 동네)
- V면 conditional (계약하면 언제부터 들어갈 수 있어요?)
- Vㄹ/을 수 있어요 (주차할 수 있어요?)
- V기 위해서 (이사하기 위해서 짐을 쌌어요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 방/거실/부엌/화장실/베란다/현관, 전세/월세/보증금/관리비/원룸, 공인중개사/계약하다/이사하다
**Cultural Note**: 전세 (jeonse) is a uniquely Korean lease structure where a tenant pays a large lump sum (typically 50–80% of property value) instead of monthly rent; the landlord invests it and returns it in full when the lease ends. Understanding this concept is essential — real-estate agents assume knowledge of it and quote both 전세 and 월세 prices.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 7, 23, 24, 31
**Rationale**: Housing is the single most important practical domain for newcomers to Korea. The 전세/월세 system is unique and opaque; learners need both vocabulary and cultural framing before they can safely conduct real-estate conversations.

## Chapter 50: Weather and Seasons

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Describe current weather and seasonal conditions, make weather-related plans, and understand forecast language.
**Scenario**: A learner checks the weekend forecast before planning a trip to Jeju, discusses typhoon warnings with a coworker, and texts a friend about canceling outdoor plans due to fine dust.
**Key Grammar**:
- A/V-(으)ㄹ 것 같아요 (weather prediction: 비가 올 것 같아요)
- V-(으)ㄹ 텐데 (I expect it will... but: 비가 올 텐데 우산 가져가세요)
- V면 (if it rains: 비가 오면 실내에서 해요)
- N 때문에 (because of fine dust: 미세먼지 때문에...)
- V(으)ㄹ 때 (when: 장마철일 때 습해요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 맑다/흐리다/비가 오다/눈이 오다/태풍/황사/미세먼지/장마/영하/기온/소나기/안개, 봄/여름/가을/겨울
**Cultural Note**: 미세먼지 (fine dust / PM2.5) has become a dominant daily-life concern in Korea, shaping whether people exercise outdoors, wear masks, or use air purifiers. Korean weather apps show a five-grade 미세먼지 index (좋음/보통/나쁨/매우나쁨/위험), and coworkers routinely start the day by checking it — more so than in most other countries.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 18, 23, 26
**Rationale**: Weather is a universal daily-life topic and a natural trigger for conditionals, predictions, and cause-effect grammar that B1 learners are actively practicing. Korea's extreme seasons (장마, typhoons, severe winters, fine-dust seasons) make weather vocabulary culturally specific and high-frequency.

## Chapter 51: Clothing and Appearance

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Shop for clothing confidently, describe outfits and personal style, and navigate Korean fashion retail language.
**Scenario**: A learner goes to a clothing store at 명동, asks about sizes and fitting rooms, returns a defective item, and later describes what to wear for a job interview to a Korean friend.
**Key Grammar**:
- V아/어 보세요 (try doing: 입어 보세요)
- V아/어 주시겠어요? (could you please: 다른 색으로 바꿔 주시겠어요?)
- pattern-132 (A-아/어 보이다: looks/seems — 너무 크게 보여요)
- A-ㄴ/은 N modifier (예쁜 원피스, 편한 신발)
- N보다 더 A (comparison: 이게 저것보다 더 잘 어울려요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 티셔츠/바지/치마/원피스/자켓/코트/운동화/구두/사이즈, 교환하다/환불하다/잘 어울리다/색깔/무늬/세일/유행/깔끔하다
**Cultural Note**: Korean retail staff are trained to greet customers at the door and may follow closely — this is attentive service, not pressure. Saying 그냥 볼게요 ('I'm just browsing') is the accepted way to browse freely. Women's clothing sizes use numeric codes (55, 66, 77) referring to old chest measurements in centimeters; knowing this avoids confusion at the fitting room.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 7, 22, 24
**Rationale**: Shopping for clothes is a high-frequency social activity in Korea, and 명동, 동대문, and online shopping platforms (무신사, 지그재그) are major cultural touchpoints for learners.

## Chapter 52: Korean Food Culture

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Discuss cooking methods and ingredients, read a restaurant menu in depth, and talk about food preferences and Korean dining customs.
**Scenario**: A learner joins Korean coworkers at a 삼겹살 restaurant, orders confidently, participates in grilling at the table, and later explains a Korean dish to a non-Korean friend.
**Key Grammar**:
- N 주세요 (ordering)
- V아/어 주세요 (please do: 조금 덜 맵게 해 주세요)
- V고 (and then: 굽고 먹어요)
- V아서/어서 (sequential/causal: 너무 매워서 못 먹겠어요)
- V으니까 (since: 저는 채식주의자니까...)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 굽다/볶다/끓이다/찌다/튀기다/간을 맞추다, 맵다/달다/짜다/신선하다, 재료/양념/국물/밑반찬/공기밥/1인분/포장해 주세요
**Cultural Note**: In Korea, 밑반찬 (side dishes) are refilled for free and unlimited at most restaurants — it is not impolite to ask for more. At 삼겹살 restaurants, the most junior person at the table typically handles the grilling, and pouring drinks for others before yourself is standard etiquette.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 7, 8, 17, 30
**Rationale**: Food is central to Korean social life and identity. The ch07 food chapter covered basic ordering vocabulary; this B1 chapter adds cooking methods, ingredient language, dining customs, and the ability to discuss taste and preference at a level that enables real social participation.

## Chapter 53: Entertainment and Digital Life

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Discuss K-dramas, K-pop, and social media using natural Korean vocabulary, and navigate smartphone and streaming app interactions.
**Scenario**: A learner recommends a K-drama to a Korean friend on KakaoTalk, discusses a K-pop idol, and asks for help with a streaming app that keeps buffering.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-085 (V(으)ㄴ 적이 있어요: have you ever... — 이 드라마 본 적 있어요?)
- A/V-는 것 같아요 (I think/seems: 이번 앨범이 제일 좋은 것 같아요)
- V-다고 했어요 (reported speech: 친구가 재밌다고 했어요)
- A/V-지만 (but/contrast: 스토리는 좋지만 좀 길어요)
- V/A-거든요 (you see/actually: 저 이 드라마 엄청 좋아하거든요)
- V/A-는데요 (softening/context-giving: 저는 좀 다른데요...)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 드라마/배우/아이돌/앨범/콘서트/덕후, 스트리밍하다/구독하다/좋아요를 누르다/댓글을 달다/공유하다, 주인공/반전/결말/자막/OST
**Cultural Note**: K-dramas are not merely entertainment; Koreans of all ages discuss current dramas at work and school the next morning as a social ritual. Saying 요즘 뭐 보세요? ('What are you watching lately?') is a standard icebreaker. K-pop fandom has its own specialized vocabulary (빠순이, 총공, 스밍, 음방) that even non-fans encounter constantly on social media.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 22, 26, 35, 37
**Rationale**: Digital entertainment is the primary cultural entry point for most Korean language learners worldwide. The specialized vocabulary of streaming, social media, and fandoms is absent from traditional textbooks but essential for authentic peer interaction in Korea.

## Chapter 54: Education and School Life

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Navigate Korean school and university contexts, discuss study habits and exams, and understand the cultural weight of academic achievement.
**Scenario**: A learner enrolls in a Korean language school, asks a classmate about the upcoming 중간고사, discusses study schedules, and explains their educational background to a Korean acquaintance.
**Key Grammar**:
- V아/어야 해요 (must: 과제를 내야 해요)
- V기로 했어요 (decided to: 도서관에서 공부하기로 했어요)
- V기 시작하다 (start doing: 공부하기 시작했어요)
- N 동안 (for a duration: 시험 기간 동안)
- V으니까 (because: 시험이 어려우니까 열심히 해야 해요)
- V기 위해서 (in order to: 합격하기 위해서 매일 공부해요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 수업/교수님/선생님/학기/중간고사/기말고사/과제/발표하다/출석하다/결석하다, 성적/장학금/전공/학원/수능/족보
**Cultural Note**: The 학원 (hagwon) system means most Korean students spend their afternoons and evenings in private academies after regular school — for English, math, science, music, and more. The 수능 (university entrance exam) is so culturally significant that flights are rerouted on the day of the listening section so noise does not disturb test-takers.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 19, 27, 31, 32
**Rationale**: Many learners in Korea are students or recent graduates, and the education domain is immediately relevant to their social context. Even non-students encounter the vocabulary constantly because Korean adults discuss school-age children, past exam experiences, and academic credentials as standard small-talk topics.

## Chapter 55: Transportation in Depth

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Navigate Korea's public transit system independently, including subway line transfers, bus routes, taxis, and KTX train booking.
**Scenario**: A learner travels from Hongik University station to Busan by KTX, using the subway to reach Seoul Station, buys a KTX ticket at the counter, and texts a friend about a delayed bus.
**Key Grammar**:
- N(으)로 가요 (go by/via: 2호선으로 환승해요)
- V(으)러 가요 (go in order to: 표 끊으러 창구에 가요)
- V(으)려고 해요 (intend to: KTX 타려고 해요)
- V면 conditional (시간이 있으면 막차 타도 돼요)
- N 때문에 (because of: 사고 때문에 지연됐어요)
- V(으)ㄹ 때 (when: 환승할 때 카드 다시 찍어요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 환승하다/노선/출구/플랫폼/막차/첫차/매진/예매하다/편도/왕복, 교통카드/충전하다/정류장/배차 간격/지연/도착 예정 시간
**Cultural Note**: Korean subway announcements name every station three times (current, next, transfer information) in Korean, English, Chinese, and Japanese. Taxis have a night surcharge (밤 할증) after 22:00, and calling a 카카오택시 is now more common than hailing on the street. The KTX uses a mandatory seat-reservation system — standing tickets exist but are not sold for all trains.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 23, 24, 31
**Rationale**: Korea's public transit system is world-class but has its own specific vocabulary and logic. Ch23 introduced direction-giving with 으로; this B1 chapter extends that to the full transit operational vocabulary needed to book, board, and navigate the system independently.

## Chapter 56: Korean Social Customs

**Level**: B1
**Goal**: Understand and participate in Korean social rituals around age, titles, gift-giving, and group harmony (눈치), avoiding common social missteps.
**Scenario**: A learner attends a Korean coworker's housewarming party (집들이), brings an appropriate gift, correctly uses titles when addressing elders, and reads the social atmosphere to know when to offer to help and when to stay quiet.
**Key Grammar**:
- V/A-잖아요 (you know how: 한국은 나이가 중요하잖아요)
- V/A-거든요 (explaining social context: 여기서는 이렇게 하거든요)
- V/A-는데요 (softening: 저는 잘 모르는데요...)
- V-(으)ㄴ/는 편이다 (tends to be: 한국 사람들은 빨리빨리 하는 편이에요)
- V으니까 (because/since: 선배니까 먼저 드세요)
- A/V-지만 (contrast: 처음에는 어색하지만 금방 친해져요)
**Vocabulary Domain**: 나이/띠/선배/후배/동갑/친해지다/어색하다, 눈치/빨리빨리/정, 집들이/세배/호칭/어르신/먼저 가세요/수고하셨습니다/덕분에/챙기다/분위기
**Cultural Note**: 눈치 (nunchi) is the Korean ability to read unspoken social cues — knowing when to speak, when to stay quiet, when to offer help without being asked, and when to leave. Koreans describe someone positively as 눈치가 빠르다 ('quick to read the room') and negatively as 눈치가 없다 ('socially oblivious'). Developing even basic nunchi as a foreigner signals genuine cultural engagement and earns significant goodwill.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 33, 34, 35, 40
**Rationale**: Grammar and vocabulary alone do not prevent social missteps. Korean social customs — the age/seniority system, 눈치, 빨리빨리 culture, specific gift-giving rituals — are sources of genuine confusion for newcomers. B1 is the right level because learners now have the grammar range to discuss abstract social norms.

## Chapter 57: Causative Verbs

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Produce and distinguish morphological causatives (사동사) from their base forms and from passive constructions, covering the seven main suffixes.
**Scenario**: A parent tells a child to eat, a teacher has students read aloud, a manager asks staff to sit — all situations where the subject causes another person to perform an action.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-147 (V-이/히/리/기/우/구/추-다: morphological causative suffixes)
- pattern-148 (V-게 하다: analytic causative — neutral indirect causation, any verb)
- pattern-149 (V-게 만들다: coercive/resultative causative — compulsion or unintended result)
- Contrast causative -히- vs passive -히-: 입히다 (dress/causative) vs 읽히다 (be read/passive) — context and carrier-marking disambiguate
**Vocabulary Domain**: Parenting and caregiving verbs (먹이다, 재우다, 입히다), education context (읽히다, 앉히다), emotional impact verbs (웃기다, 울리다), service verbs (알리다, 낮추다)
**Cultural Note**: The causative -게 만들다 often appears in Korean emotional discourse and media commentary: 사람을 감동하게 만드는 영화 ('a film that moves people'). This construction is ubiquitous in film/drama reviews and social media opinions about entertainment.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 38, 39
**Rationale**: The seven morphological causative suffixes are a closed, high-frequency set. Without explicit instruction, B2 learners either avoid them entirely (defaulting to 게 하다) or confuse them with passive forms sharing the same suffixes. This chapter consolidates the full paradigm with contrastive passive drilling.

## Chapter 58: 겠 — Volition, Inference, and Softened Speech

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Use 겠 accurately to express the speaker's volitional intent, confident inference from evidence, and social softening across registers, distinguishing it from -(으)ㄹ 거예요.
**Scenario**: A waiter promises to bring an order, a scientist infers a result from data, and a colleague politely declines — all three using 겠 for stylistically distinct reasons.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-150 (V-겠어요/겠습니다: 1st-person in-the-moment commitment, formal promise)
- pattern-151 (A/V-겠네요/겠군요: evidential inference — confident inference from visible evidence)
- 겠 as social softener: 어렵겠습니다 (politely declining), 알겠어요 (fixed understanding expression)
- Contrast: 겠 vs -(으)ㄹ 거예요 (plan/prediction) vs -(으)ㄹ 것 같아요 (uncertain guess)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Service and formal commitment verbs (처리하다/도와드리다/확인하다), inference about observable situations (피곤하다/힘들다/바쁘다/어렵다), polite refusal expressions
**Cultural Note**: In Korean service contexts, using -(으)ㄹ 거예요 instead of -겠습니다 for a promise sounds like a casual prediction rather than a committed service promise. A customer service agent who says 도와줄 거예요 sounds informal; 도와드리겠습니다 is the expected form. This is a real-world register marker, not merely a stylistic preference.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 18, 26, 33
**Rationale**: 겠 is among the highest-frequency modal morphemes in Korean but is taught only incidentally in most curricula. Its three distinct pragmatic functions (volition, inference, softening) require dedicated instruction because they map onto different epistemic and social positions.

## Chapter 59: Unreal Conditionals

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Construct counterfactual conditionals using -았/었으면, -았/었더라면, and -지 않았더라면 to express regret, hypotheticals, and contrary-to-fact wishes.
**Scenario**: A student reflects on a failed exam, a traveler imagines an alternate route, and a friend expresses what should have been said differently — all requiring counterfactual framing.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-152 (V-았/었으면 좋겠어요: past counterfactual wish)
- pattern-153 (V-았/었더라면 … -았/었을 텐데: strong counterfactual conditional)
- V-지 않았더라면/V-지 않았으면: negated counterfactual
- Contrast with open conditional -(으)면 (ch11): present 으면 = 'if/when (possibly real)', past 았/었으면 = 'if only I had/were (unmet)'
- Result clause must also be hypothetical: -았/었을 텐데 or -았/었을 거예요
**Vocabulary Domain**: Regret and reflection vocabulary (포기하다/실패하다/성공하다/도와주다), missed opportunities (일찍/늦게/미리), life decisions and alternate paths
**Cultural Note**: Koreans use the phrase 그때로 돌아갈 수 있다면... ('If I could go back to that time...') very commonly in reflective speech. Counterfactual reasoning is embedded in Korean conversational culture — advice-giving, empathizing with others' regrets, and self-reflection all rely heavily on these structures.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 25, 26, 40
**Rationale**: Counterfactual conditionals are structurally distinct from open conditionals in Korean. The -았/었으면 vs -으면 contrast is a consistent source of learner error at B2. The literary -았/었더라면 form is required for reading Korean media, drama scripts, and formal writing.

## Chapter 60: Nominalisation — 기 vs 는 것

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Choose correctly between the -기 nominaliser and the -는 것 nominaliser based on semantic weight, definiteness, and grammatical environment.
**Scenario**: A language tutor explains how to study, a doctor discusses healthy habits, and a journalist describes a recurring event — each requiring a different nominaliser.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-154 (V-기 vs V-는 것: nominalisation contrast — abstract/predicate-selected vs specific/referential)
- V-기 required collocations: 기로 하다 (decide), 기는 하다 (concessive), 기 때문에 (because of doing), 기 위해서 (in order to), 기 전에 (before doing), 기 시작하다 (start doing)
- V-는 것 required contexts: after 알다/모르다/이해하다/보다 as the known/seen thing; when the nominalised clause is topicalized with 은/는
- 기 어렵다 / 기 쉽다 (evaluative adjective frames require 기, NOT 는 것이 어렵다)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Abstract activities (운동하다/공부하다/걷다/말하다), predicate-selecting verbs (좋아하다/싫어하다/시작하다/그만두다), knowledge/perception verbs (알다/모르다/보다/느끼다)
**Cultural Note**: Korean academic and business writing relies heavily on nominalization to compress information: 이 문제를 해결하기 위해 ('in order to solve this problem') is the standard opening for formal proposals. Mastering nominalisation is the gateway to formal written Korean competency.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 21, 27, 28, 32
**Rationale**: The 기 / 는 것 contrast is a persistent production error even at B2. Learners default to 는 것 everywhere because it appears more transparent, creating unnatural sentences after evaluative adjectives and predicate-selected complement verbs.

## Chapter 61: Sentence-Final Attitude Endings

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Deploy the expressive sentence-final endings 네요, 군요/구나, -(으)ㄹ걸요, and -았/었을 텐데 to signal speaker attitude — realisation, surprise, retrospective regret, and unfulfilled expectation.
**Scenario**: A student just learned something unexpected, a friend wishes a plan had gone differently, and a colleague gently hints they were right all along — all using attitude endings rather than plain statements.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-155 (A/V-네요: fresh realisation, direct sensory discovery — obligatorily first-realization)
- pattern-156 (A/V-군요/V-는군요: exclamatory polite surprise, more intellectual/deductive)
- pattern-157 (V-(으)ㄹ걸요: mild regret / confident hedged assertion — dual function)
- pattern-158 (V-았/었을 텐데: unfulfilled expectation / gentle trailing regret)
- -구나 (casual counterpart of 군요, for plain speech or talking to oneself)
**Vocabulary Domain**: Realisation and discovery verbs (알다/모르다/보다), surprise-triggering situations, regret contexts (놓치다/포기하다/늦다), expectation-setting adjectives (당연하다/예상하다)
**Cultural Note**: In Korean conversation, sentence-final attitude endings function like vocal tone in English — they tell the listener how to interpret what you are saying. Using 가요 (neutral) vs 가네요 (I just noticed you're going!) vs 갔을 텐데 (you must have already gone) carries completely different pragmatic meanings. Misreading these cues leads to significant miscommunication.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 26, 36, 40
**Rationale**: Sentence-final attitude endings are pervasive in spoken and written Korean but are often treated as optional stylistic additions. At B2, learners must use them productively to sound natural and to interpret native speaker tone accurately.

## Chapter 62: Topic vs Subject — 은/는 and 이/가

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Choose between 은/는 and 이/가 to correctly signal topic continuity, new information, contrast, emphasis, and exhaustive listing in discourse-level Korean.
**Scenario**: A narrator introduces a new character, then contrasts two people's opinions, then explains a fact about one of them exclusively — moving through the full topic-subject contrast space.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-159 (N이/가 vs N은/는: discourse contrast — new information/exhaustive identification vs topic/contrast/given)
- N이/가: new to discourse; answers an implicit 'who/what' question; exhaustively identifies
- N은/는: discourse-given; explicit contrast ('A but B'); general truths about a topic
- Predicates requiring 이/가 regardless of given-ness: 있다/없다, 되다, 아니다
- Double-marked NPs: N은/는 (topic frame) + N이/가 (new information within frame) — 코끼리는 코가 길어요
- 누가 했어요? → 제가 했어요 (NOT 저는 했어요) — 이/가 for exhaustive identification
**Vocabulary Domain**: Narrative vocabulary, comparative description, news/announcement contexts where introduction and contrast alternate
**Cultural Note**: The 은/는 vs 이/가 choice is grammatically visible in Korean writing but invisible in English, which relies on intonation and word order to signal the same distinctions. Korean learners whose first language is English must learn to make an explicit grammatical choice for something English encodes prosodically.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 3, 4, 35
**Rationale**: The 은/는 vs 이/가 choice is one of the most described yet persistently unmastered contrasts in Korean pedagogy. At B2, learners can produce grammatical sentences with either particle but still make pragmatically inappropriate choices. This chapter provides the information-structure framework (given/new, topic/focus, contrast/exhaustive) that makes the choice principled.

## Chapter 63: Advanced Written Korean

**Level**: C1
**Goal**: Read and produce formal written Korean using discourse connectors (따라서, 그러나, 반면에, 즉, 결국, 한편) and nominative/copula patterns distinctive to formal prose.
**Scenario**: Writing a formal complaint, summarising a news article, or composing an essay argument — all requiring the written register that differs substantially from spoken 해요체.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-160 (따라서/그러므로: formal conclusion connectors — replace spoken 그래서)
- pattern-161 (그러나/반면에/한편: formal contrast and parallel connectors)
- pattern-162 (즉/다시 말해서: written clarification and restatement connectors)
- pattern-163 (결국/궁극적으로: culmination and logical conclusion markers)
- Written-register features: -다 plain form predicates (instead of -아/어요), nominalized subjects, passive constructions with -어지다/에 의해
**Vocabulary Domain**: Academic and journalistic discourse vocabulary: 가설/이론/결론/근거/반론/논리/사례, news reporting terms (발표하다/주장하다/분석하다/검토하다)
**Cultural Note**: Korean formal writing uses the -다/-ㄴ다 plain predicate form, not the polite -아/어요 form used in speech. This is a systematic register switch — a single paragraph mixing 해요체 endings with formal discourse connectors reads as stylistically incoherent to a Korean reader. The switch to -다 endings signals that the text is objective/reportorial rather than personal/conversational.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 33, 35, 40
**Rationale**: Formal written Korean uses a set of discourse connectors largely absent from spoken textbook curricula. Without explicit instruction, C1 learners transfer spoken connectors (그래서, 그런데) into academic and journalistic writing, producing texts that read as informal. This chapter bridges spoken B2 fluency to written C1 competency.

## Chapter 64: Korean Internet Language and Colloquial Intensifiers

**Level**: B2
**Goal**: Recognise and appropriately use Korean internet abbreviations, phonetic shortenings, and colloquial intensifiers in informal digital communication while understanding their register constraints.
**Scenario**: Reading a social media comment thread, replying to a friend's message on KakaoTalk, and understanding a streaming chat during a live broadcast.
**Key Grammar**:
- pattern-164 (ㅋㅋ/ㅠㅠ/ㅇㅇ: consonant emoticons — laughter, distress, affirmation)
- pattern-165 (완전/진짜/대박: colloquial intensifiers and exclamations — grammaticalised in informal speech)
- 헐 (shocked/deadpan disbelief), 어머 (female-coded surprise exclamation), 대박 (wow/amazement)
- 너무 dual register: 너무 비싸요 (standard: too expensive) vs 너무 좋아 (informal: really love it)
- Phonetic shortenings: 뭐야 → 머, -(이)잖아 → -(이)잖, ㅇㅇ for 응/네
- Register awareness: these forms belong strictly to peer informal / digital communication
**Vocabulary Domain**: Digital communication vocabulary (스트리밍/댓글/공유하다/좋아요), K-pop/K-drama fan vocabulary (스밍/음방/총공), emoji and abbreviation conventions
**Cultural Note**: Korean internet language evolves very rapidly — new slang terms from idol fan communities, memes, and TV shows enter daily digital communication within days. But even as vocabulary shifts, the consonant emoticon system (ㅋㅋ, ㅠㅠ, ㅇㅇ) and core intensifiers (완전, 진짜, 대박) remain stable anchors of digital-register Korean.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: 33, 35, 53
**Rationale**: Korean internet language is a distinct register that learners encounter from day one of immersion but that structured curricula almost never address. B2 learners living in Korea or consuming Korean media are immediately exposed to ㅋㅋ and consonant-only typing without any pedagogical framework.

## Chapter 65: Synthesis and Production Review

**Level**: B2/C1
**Goal**: Demonstrate mastery of all patterns ch1–64 through integrated production tasks spanning multiple registers, domains, and grammar points simultaneously.
**Scenario**: A learner prepares for a job interview in Korea, writes a formal email, has a casual conversation with coworkers, and sends a casual KakaoTalk message — all in the same day, switching registers appropriately.
**Key Grammar**:
- Register switching: 합쇼체 (formal) / 해요체 (polite everyday) / 반말 (casual) / digital-register — all four deployed appropriately by context
- Extended discourse: connecting 5+ clauses using the full range of clause connectors learned across ch1–64
- Counterfactual and hypothetical layers: combining 았/었더라면, -겠-, and 텐데 in the same paragraph
- new pattern needed: synthesis frame — production checklist reviewing: particle accuracy, irregular conjugation, register appropriateness, nominalisation choice, discourse connector selection
**Vocabulary Domain**: Cross-domain review: incorporates health, housing, food, education, transport, social customs, entertainment, and digital vocabulary from ch48–64
**Cultural Note**: The ability to switch registers fluidly — from formal 합쇼체 in a job interview to casual digital language with friends later that day — is the hallmark of true B2 Korean competency. Monolingual English speakers often underestimate how much of Korean communicative competence is register choice rather than vocabulary or grammar.
**Prerequisite Chapters**: All chapters 1–64
**Rationale**: A synthesis chapter serves as a curriculum capstone, providing learners with a structured self-assessment opportunity and giving instructors a holistic evaluation point. It also models the real-world task of integrating all competencies simultaneously rather than applying them in isolated grammar exercises.
